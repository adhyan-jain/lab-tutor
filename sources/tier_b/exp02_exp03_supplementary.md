## Experiment 2 — Ester hydrolysis kinetics (p.16–19)

Introduction (p.16): chemical kinetics is the part of physical chemistry that studies reaction rates. Studying it shows how to change reaction conditions to improve a rate, and how to suppress or slow unwanted side reactions. For a generic reaction A + B giving C the simple rate equation is the rate constant times the concentration of A raised to a power a times the concentration of B raised to a power b. The rate constant is not really a constant, because it includes everything that affects the rate other than concentration: mainly temperature, ionic strength, surface area of an adsorbent, or light irradiation for photochemical reactions. The exponents a and b are the reaction orders and depend on the reaction mechanism; the stoichiometric coefficients and the orders are very often equal, but only in one-step reactions. The hydrolysis of an ester is catalysed by an acid or a base. The most common method is to use a Lewis acid or a Bronsted acid to form a positively charged intermediate that is far more reactive, so even mild nucleophiles such as water will react.

Aim and principle (p.17): the aim is to determine the rate constant of the hydrolysis of ethyl acetate using an acid as a catalyst, and to understand the order and molecularity of the reaction. The problem is the hydrolysis of ethyl acetate in acid medium to give acetic acid and ethyl alcohol; the method is following the kinetics by acid-base titrimetric analysis; the formation of acetic acid is followed by titration with NaOH at different time intervals. The reaction is CH3COOC2H5 + H2O, with H+ as catalyst, giving CH3COOH + C2H5OH. The rate is proportional to the ester concentration times the water concentration; since the water concentration remains constant, the rate equals minus dc/dt equals k1' times the ester concentration, where c is the ester concentration at time t and k1' is the specific velocity constant. As the reaction progresses acetic acid accumulates, so drawing a known volume of the reaction mixture at regular intervals and titrating it against standard sodium hydroxide shows the increase in acetic acid. The manual states that the acid hydrolysis of an ester is a first-order bimolecular reaction that follows pseudo first-order kinetics, because the amount of water is in large excess so its concentration does not change significantly. The reaction goes practically to completion (the equilibrium shifts to the right) and the rate is first order with respect to the ester.

Requirements (p.17–18): reagents and solutions are ethyl acetate, 0.5 N HCl, 0.2 N NaOH, phenolphthalein indicator and ice cubes. Apparatus is a 50 mL burette, 5 mL and 10 mL pipettes, conical flasks, a wash bottle and a 250 mL reaction bottle.

Procedure (p.18): exactly 100 mL of 0.5 N hydrochloric acid are taken in a clean 250 mL reaction bottle and exactly 5 mL of the ester is added to it. Zero time is noted when half the volume of ester solution in the pipette has been transferred into the reaction bottle. After thorough mixing, immediately 10 mL of the solution is pipetted out into a clean conical flask containing ice cubes. It is then titrated against 0.2 N sodium hydroxide from the burette using phenolphthalein indicator; the end-point is the first appearance of a pale permanent pink colour. The same volume of reaction mixture is withdrawn at regular intervals, say 10 minutes, and titrated against sodium hydroxide. The reaction is allowed to go to completion by keeping the reaction mixture over a hot water bath for about 90 minutes, and the final reading is then noted.

Calculations (p.18): let V0 be the volume of alkali used at zero time, Vt the volume used after time t, and V-infinity the reading when the reaction is completed. Concentrations of the ester at various times are expressed in terms of volume of NaOH solution. The initial concentration of ester, a, is V-infinity minus V0. The concentration of ester at time t, a minus x, is V-infinity minus Vt. The specific rate constant is k1' equals 2.303 over t times the logarithm (base 10) of a over (a minus x), which is 2.303 over t times the logarithm of (V-infinity minus V0) over (V-infinity minus Vt). Equivalently, k1' equals the slope multiplied by 2.303. The rate constant values calculated at the different time intervals should be nearly the same. A graph is drawn between the logarithm of (V-infinity minus Vt) and time t; from the slope of the plot the rate constant is calculated and compared with the experimental value.

Table and results (p.19): Table 1 has a row for each of the times 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 minutes and a final row for infinity, with columns for the time in minutes, the volume of NaOH in mL, a second volume column in mL, and the rate constant in per minute. The cells are blank for the student to fill. The Results section asks for the rate constant for the hydrolysis of the ester (1) as a calculated value and (2) as a graphical value, and for (3) the molecularity of the reaction and (4) the order of the reaction. Marks are 2 for the experiment, 2 for observations and calculations and 6 for results, out of 10.

## Experiment 3 — Colorimetric estimation of Ni2+ (p.20–23)

Background (p.20): nickel is a transition element that commonly exists in the +2 oxidation state, though +1, +3 and +4 also occur in nickel complexes. It has an important role in biological systems as a constituent of several enzymes, and is present in soils and plants, where its concentration varies from trace amounts to a major constituent, so determining nickel in a variety of samples is important. Compared with other transition metals nickel is moderately toxic, but inhaling nickel and its compounds can cause serious problems including cancer of the respiratory system, and nickel can cause a skin disorder known as nickel eczema. A thin layer of nickel on a metal object can be decorative, or give corrosion resistance or wear resistance, or be used to build up worn parts. Nickel alloys are used extensively for their corrosion resistance, high-temperature strength and special magnetic and thermal expansion properties; the manual lists iron-nickel-chromium alloys, stainless steels, copper-nickel and nickel-copper alloys, nickel-chromium alloys, nickel-chromium-iron alloys, low expansion alloys and magnetic alloys.

Problem, method and solution (p.21): corrosion protection in steel depends on the amount of nickel, which acts as a passivating metal, so it is important to analyse the amount of Ni in steel for industrial use. Ni-DMG forms a stable coloured complex; as the concentration of nickel in solution increases, the colour intensity increases, and the colour intensity is in turn a function of the colour coordinates (red, green and blue, RGB) of an image taken with a mobile phone camera. The concentration of nickel in the unknown sample is estimated from a calibration graph plotted from known nickel concentrations.

Principle (p.21): (a) Colorimetric method. Photo-sensitive measurements are expressed as absorbance A, and the relation is epsilon times c times l equals A equals log(I0 over I), where I0 is the incident light power, I the transmitted light power, epsilon the molar absorptivity, c the concentration of the analyte and l the thickness of the solution. (b) Digital-imaging method. The colour and intensity of a digital image are usually 24-bit data (8 bit red, 8 bit green and 8 bit blue) forming an additive colour space in which R, G and B lights are added in various combinations to reproduce a broad range of colours. Each colour has 256 levels from 0 to 255; R = 0, G = 0, B = 0 is pure black and R = 255, G = 255, B = 255 is pure white. The goal is to use digital-image colorimetry to determine Ni2+ in aqueous samples; the concentration of the analyte is a function of the colour coordinates.

Reagents, solutions and instrumentation (p.21): NiSO4 (100 ppm), NaOH (1 N) solution, dimethylglyoxime (DMG), K3[Fe(CN)6], a colorimeter and a smartphone.

Reaction scheme (p.21–22): DMG reacts with Ni2+ to form a pink Ni(dmg)2 complex in alkaline medium, which is oxidised by potassium ferricyanide, K3[Fe(CN)6], to a brown-red, water-soluble oxidised Ni(dmg)2 complex (Scheme 1). The absorption spectrum of the oxidised complex has its absorption maximum at 440 nm (Fig. 1). The concentration of Ni2+ in the unknown is determined from the calibration graph (Fig. 2). The scheme and the two figures are shown only as images in the source.

Procedure, colorimetry method (p.22): take five standard 50 mL volumetric flasks, to prepare four known solutions and one unknown. Fill the burette with the nickel stock solution (100 ppm). Add 1, 2, 3 and 4 mL of the nickel solution from the burette to the standard flasks to get 2, 4, 6 and 8 ppm nickel solutions. The unknown sample is furnished in another 50 mL volumetric flask. Then add 0.5 mL of DMG solution followed by 0.5 mL of K3[Fe(CN)6] solution, using a burette, to all five flasks. All the flasks are shaken well once and left for 5 minutes. After that, make up to the 50 mL mark with 1 N NaOH solution. Allow the flasks at least 10 minutes for complete complex formation. The absorbance of the formed brown-red solution is measured at 440 nm against NaOH solution as the blank, and the readings are recorded in Table 1. Draw a calibration graph with the concentration of Ni2+ in ppm on the X-axis and absorbance on the Y-axis. A straight line passing through the origin (Fig. 2) indicates that the measured data obey Beer's law. From the calibration plot, read off the concentration of nickel in the given unknown sample.

Procedure, digital imaging method (p.22–23): the prepared standard solutions are lined up along with the unknown sample and the blank. With white paper as the background, take a photograph of the samples holding the camera about 50 cm away. The calibration curve is built from the RGB values of the analytical response at different Ni2+ concentrations using the "RGB Tool" app, and the RGB response varies linearly with the analyte concentration. The manual's flow chart is: transfer the prepared standard solutions and the unknown into different colorimetric test tubes; take an image of all the test-tube solutions with a smartphone camera; open the image-processing app (an RGB colour detector); go to the gallery, open the stored image in the app and extract the RGB values for each image and concentration; process the RGB values as the ratios R/G, R/B or G/B and so on until a linear response is obtained; plot the calibration curve of the RGB linear response against concentration; and find the unknown concentration from the calibration curve.

Table and results (p.23): Table 1 has five rows, four standards and the unknown. For the colorimetric device it records the concentration in ppm (X-axis) and the absorbance (Y-axis); for the smartphone device it records R, G and B and the ratios R/G, G/B and R/B. A note says the ratio that increases linearly with analyte concentration is the one used for plotting the calibration figure. The Results section asks for the concentration of Ni in the steel sample in ppm (mg/L) (i) using colorimetry and (ii) using digital imaging. Marks are 2 for the experiment, 2 for observations and calculations and 6 for results, out of 10.
